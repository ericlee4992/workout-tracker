# Consistent exercise animations for Stacked — six proposals

Independent advice, 2026-10-18. Read against the exercise-media spec, reference UX study, both licensing rounds,
and the actual SeedCatalog.json. This proposes work; no media was purchased, generated, downloaded or commissioned.
Published facts are linked; coverage projections, effort and budgets below are estimates, not supplier quotes.

**Recommendation:** first test a small, carefully matched Gym visual purchase, conditional on written permission
for our delivery model. It is the shortest route to the requested consistent 3D look. If its terms or coverage fail,
build one original Blender figure and machine-animation pipeline. If zero asset-license spending is the overriding
constraint, an original 2.5D motion-diagram style is more credible than promising 90 polished AI videos for free.

## What makes this particular catalog difficult

The seed has **90 exercises**: 57 carry a machine tag, 7 cable and 2 Smith; accounting for overlap, **61 have at least
one of those tags and 29 have none**. This describes our catalog, not confirmed vendor coverage. There are also
**1,877 equipment-model records**. Ninety generic movement demonstrations therefore cannot claim to demonstrate
all those physical machines exactly.

Several names need a more precise movement brief before any source can satisfy them: Rotator Cuff, Neck Machine,
Multi-Hip, Machine Squat and Grip Trainer. For example, neck flexion and extension need different demonstrations.
A barbell hack squat is not a hack-squat machine; a landmine attachment is not automatically our Viking press.
Do not accept fuzzy name matches as coverage or pool these distinctions into one reassuring-looking clip.

There is also a hard limit to “every custom exercise”: an arbitrary name does not specify enough movement to
produce a correct demonstration. Every approach below can give immediate media to a custom exercise that explicitly
selects an existing, reviewed movement. A genuinely new movement needs an authored/reviewed asset. If immediate
animation for every custom entry is mandatory, creation must require that movement selection; otherwise allow an
honest pending-media state. Never quietly assign the nearest-looking exercise.

The own-drawn muscle maps remain the decision. They can use the same visual vocabulary as our animations, but
animation production does not require reopening the map decision. Highlights should be reviewed target-muscle
illustrations, not pretend measurements of activation.

## Ranking and realistic economics

Ranking weighs the requested look, machine coverage, time to ship, control and offline operation together.
All six can produce files played without a runtime AI service. Rights must authorize the chosen distribution.

| Rank | Approach | Route to all 90 | Source/production cost and effort estimate | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Licensed coherent clips, with supplier-made gaps | Audit 90 exact mappings; buy missing variants or commission the same supplier | Indicative $81 GIF / $540 video library arithmetic; 25–50 hours curation/conversion; gaps and permission terms unquoted | Delivery terms need clarification; 90 exact matches not established |
| 2 | Original Blender rig, keyframed and procedural machines | Author and approve every movement; reusable equipment families | $0 software/license cost if entirely original; roughly 200–500 production hours | Machine geometry and form authoring dominate |
| 3 | Original code-drawn 2.5D motion diagrams | Author 90 constrained pose sequences in one visual language | $0 asset fees; roughly 100–240 engineering/authoring hours | A different look, with less depth/anatomical detail |
| 4 | Commission a coach/studio to perform; animate our figure | Capture on the required machines, then clean and retarget | $1,000–$4,000 capture allowance plus 150–350 animation hours | Machine access, occlusion and cleanup; requires contracts |
| 5 | Direct manufacturer/studio partnership | Partners supply licensed machine references/assets; one renderer unifies them | Reserve $0–$5,000 for a pilot negotiation, not a quoted price; 40–120 coordination/integration hours plus unfinished animation work | A partner may decline; no full-library coverage guarantee |
| 6 | AI-generated video from our own approved inputs | Each of 90 must pass individual review; failures return to manual animation | Example $225–$900 generation credits plus inputs, retries beyond budget and 60–160 cleanup/curation hours | Highest risk of plausible-looking wrong motion |

These are **production planning ranges**, not measured throughput. Commissioning 200 hours at an assumed $60/hour
would be $12,000; doing it ourselves makes the cash cost lower, not the work disappear. Supplier quotes should replace
these assumptions after a pilot. Add common app integration and independent form review to every route: initially
allow roughly 40–80 developer hours for the shared player/cache/mapping/UI, and 10–25 reviewer hours plus corrections.
At a deliberately assumed $50–$75 reviewer hourly rate, that is $500–$1,875 for review, not a market-rate claim.
No estimate includes tax, legal review, unusual machine travel or a guaranteed full-library delivery date.

## 1. Buy a coherent library, commission only its gaps

**How and look.** Make an exercise-ID-to-product mapping first; choose one figure/style/camera convention. Purchase
licensed assets, keep the originals private, review each movement and machine match, make app-sized derivatives
where authorized, and publish one versioned media pack. Ask the original supplier to produce missing movements in
its existing style. This gives the closest match to the desired 3D figure with tinted muscles without building a rig.
A uniform player frame alone will not make unrelated artwork consistent.

**Coverage.** There is concrete evidence beyond basic free-weight lifts: Gym visual lists a six-second
[pendulum-squat video](https://gymvisual.com/videos/11107-lever-pendulum-squat-plate-loaded.html), a
[Viking-press video](https://gymvisual.com/videos/19892-lever-standing-viking-press-male.html), and a
[belt-squat GIF](https://gymvisual.com/animated-gifs/6763-lever-belt-squat.html). These are candidate products, not
verified matches to all our machines. Neck-machine direction, Multi-Hip, Rotator Cuff and the row variants still
need item-by-item decisions. I have not established 90/90 coverage.

**Cost.** The [catalog](https://gymvisual.com/13-media-content?%3Fp%3D9=&p=651) advertises $0.90/GIF after ten items
and $6/video after five. Ninety would therefore be $81 or $540 before tax and gaps, assuming eligible exact matches.
The belt-squat product instead shows a five-item 75% discount from $3.60; confirm the actual basket and resolution.
Those figures are license arithmetic, not an all-in project quote. Prefer a usable 720p source over cheap 180p art
that becomes unreadable on the exercise page. GIF-to-MP4 conversion cannot recover missing frames or detail.

**Rights.** The [license](https://gymvisual.com/content/9-license) expressly permits iOS app illustration and grants
perpetual use of purchased media; it retains copyright, bars redistribution for reuse and prohibits AI processing or
generative reuse. Its [separate terms](https://gymvisual.com/content/3-terms-and-conditions-of-use) also restrict
on-demand services and reserve removal/revocation powers. This qualifies the earlier report's optimistic R2 inference.
Before adopting this route, obtain a written grant covering bundling, app-only R2 delivery, persistent offline copies,
transcoding/posters, contractor access and continued use. A private bucket is not itself permission. No AI restyling
of these clips, no public source files, and no preview assets in TestFlight.

**Accuracy, offline and custom exercises.** Licensed does not mean form-reviewed. Check joint path, setup and the
actual mechanism. With the agreed storage grant, bundle or retain downloaded files for offline playback. Custom
exercises may explicitly reuse a licensed demonstration within our app's agreed scope; a new movement requires a
new purchase or supplier commission. Do not let users export licensed raw assets.

**First experiment.** Audit ten hard representatives: bench press, deadlift, seated row, lat pulldown, leg extension,
pec deck, hack squat, pendulum squat, Viking press and a specifically defined neck-machine movement. Obtain the
permission answer before a purchase/delivery prototype. Test purchased examples on a phone-sized player and in
Airplane Mode. Reject the route if the supplier cannot authorize offline distribution or the exact machine variants
cannot be sourced coherently. Missing stock items are a commissioning cost, not license-free substitutions.

## 2. Own a Blender figure and author the motion, including machine mechanisms

**How and look.** Commission or create one original stylized mannequin, rig, muscle-color regions, lighting and
camera template. Build reusable equipment families: seated lever, cable/pulley, linear sled, pendulum, Smith rail,
bench and free weights. For each exercise, an animator and form reviewer define setup, contact points, movement
phases and useful range. Constrain hands/feet to handles/platforms; keyframe the motion and equipment together;
render a seamless approximately six-second MP4 and poster. Batch rendering is code; deciding correct movement is not.
One camera/rig/material system gives actual visual consistency and can generate front/side variants later.

**Coverage and effort.** All 90 are authorable in principle once ambiguous names are specified. Machine families
share geometry and tooling, not necessarily trajectories: a pendulum squat and sled hack squat remain separate
rigs. Budget 200–500 person-hours for an original first library, then measure the pilot's repeatable per-exercise
cost. Blender has no software fee; outsourced labor is the dominant cost. An original rig avoids asset-license
fees and gives the best long-term control over custom machines.

**Rights.** Blender's GPL does not impose GPL on original artwork made with it; imported assets retain their own
conditions. [Blender licensing](https://www.blender.org/about/license/). Use original/assigned models and scripts,
with a written asset-provenance inventory. Do not call downloaded rigs or motions ours exclusively.

**Possible accelerator, not the machine solution.** [Adobe's Mixamo FAQ](https://helpx.adobe.com/creative-cloud/faq/mixamo-faq.html)
permits commercial projects with its characters/motions. The researched
[Fab barbell pack](https://www.fab.com/listings/2ac3a2de-3497-47e3-9f9a-203712fbca7d) can reduce some barbell authoring,
subject to its selected license; the earlier $18–$24 price was not re-confirmed. Its barbell “Hack Squat” does not
prove machine coverage. Conventional retargeting may help the non-machine portion; it will not supply our 61
machine/cable/Smith-tagged entries. Keep restricted source assets private and obey any no-AI restrictions.

**Accuracy, offline and custom exercises.** Constraint-based motion avoids drifting handles and changing limb
lengths, but a mathematically smooth path can still teach bad form. Review key poses and the interpolated frames.
Pre-rendered loops play fully offline; Blender never ships inside the app. Custom exercises reuse approved
movement definitions or get a new authored sequence, rendered in the same style.

**First experiment.** Spend at most 20–40 authoring hours on one bench press, one seated leg extension and one
pendulum squat using the same figure. Reject the approach if fixing contacts, occlusion or machine motion repeatedly
requires rebuilding the rig, or the user dislikes the render. Continue only with a measured per-clip estimate and
editable source delivery. Do not approve the pipeline from a squat-only demo.

## 3. Draw our own 2.5D movement diagrams in code

**How and look.** Use a deliberately diagrammatic figure: consistent filled limb shapes, joint hierarchy, limited
perspective and simple equipment silhouettes. Author joint-angle keyframes plus explicit equipment movement,
contact constraints and front/back layer order. Render with native SwiftUI Canvas or pre-render the same geometry
to video. Apple provides [Canvas for immediate-mode drawing](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/drawing-and-graphics),
so this requires no third-party runtime. The look is a clean moving instruction diagram in Floodlight colors,
not a photorealistic anatomical person. Original muscle patches can move with the limb geometry.

**Coverage and effort.** All 90 can have purpose-authored diagrams, but not necessarily a convincing single-view
representation: pec deck, torso rotation, adduction and neck directions may need another view. Start with roughly
12–20 reusable motion/mechanism families and expect exercise-specific overrides. That is a design hypothesis,
not “twenty templates automatically cover ninety exercises.” Allow 100–240 hours for renderer, tooling, poses and
machine diagrams; asset fees can be $0. Code generation can help build the renderer, but cannot certify its poses.

**Rights and accuracy.** Use our own geometry and authored movement parameters, or assets with an explicit suitable
grant. Do not trace someone else's frames. Smoothly morphing two Everkinetic poses would neither prove correct
intermediate motion nor make an original animation: licensed adaptations retain their obligations.
[CC BY-SA terms](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/). The main risk is concealing depth or joint rotation
in a simplified silhouette. A loop that looks clean but cannot communicate the machine path fails the goal.

**Offline and custom exercises.** Bundle small pose/geometry data; native playback works immediately without network
or video downloads. Approved custom variants select a family and reviewed preset. Do not expose unrestricted joint
sliders or generate a motion solely from an exercise name as if it were verified instruction.

**First experiment.** Build side-view leg extension and three-quarter pec deck, then a pendulum squat, all using one
visual language. At the actual detail-sheet size, a trainer must identify hand/foot placement and machine travel
without explanatory rescue text. Reject if those motions require misleading projections or the user requires the
3D mannequin look. This is my preferred experiment if asset spending must stay at zero.

## 4. Pay someone else to perform the lifts; render those motions on our figure

**How and look.** Hire a qualified demonstrator and a gym/studio with the required machines. They shoot original
front/side reference takes under our brief; the user does not film or provide footage. An animator uses those
licensed originals to keyframe or, where suitable, extract and clean poses, then applies them to our rig and
simplified machine models. Render every exercise with identical materials and camera treatment. The shipped result
is animation; the real-person footage is production reference, not a visual patchwork in the app.

**Coverage and cost.** Broad coverage depends on physical access. A normal gym may have leg extension and pec deck
but no pendulum/belt squat, Viking press or neck machine; get an equipment inventory before booking. Reserve
$1,000–$4,000 for a small multi-day capture effort, then 150–350 hours for cleanup/animation. These are bid-request
allowances, not quotes; travel and rare equipment may exceed them. Existing option-2 rigs reduce duplication.

**Rights.** Require a contract covering the footage, motion-derived data, final renders, performer releases,
location permissions, edits and commercial offline app distribution. Acquire source files and an assignment or
sufficient perpetual license. Paying a contractor alone is not a reliable ownership arrangement; US work-for-hire
rules depend on defined conditions and written agreements. [US Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/register/se-hire.html).

**Accuracy, offline and custom exercises.** Real performance gives stronger reference than invented frames, but
an athlete can perform incorrectly and pose extraction can lose a hip or hand behind a pad. Review the final render,
not only the take. Assets work offline once bundled/downloaded. Commission new custom movements in batches; routine
variants can reuse an existing reviewed demonstration. This is not an immediate answer for arbitrary user creations.

**First experiment.** Commission only a leg extension, seated row and one difficult specialty machine. Require the
originals, cleaned motion and rendered loop. Stop if contact/occlusion cleanup is no cheaper than manual keyframing,
or equipment/location/performer rights cannot be documented. No YouTube extraction is involved.

## 5. Partner directly with equipment makers or an exercise-animation studio

**How and look.** Approach a maker/studio with a specific offer: its approved machine setup becomes a correctly
identified example in Stacked; we receive licensed CAD/reference geometry and, ideally, approved movement data or
custom renders. Use our single figure/material/camera template to produce the finished loops. If a partner only
offers branded videos without adaptation rights, those do not meet the consistency requirement. A directory of
mixed manufacturers' tutorials is a different product.

**Coverage and cost.** This is attractive for the difficult machine portion and our large equipment-model catalog,
but one partner rarely covers the whole exercise list. Complete common movements through option 2, while partners
supply selected machine-specific packs. Budget 40–120 hours for negotiation, mapping and integration, plus the actual
animation work. A $0–$5,000 pilot allowance can test interest; no free grant, rate or willing partner has been verified.
Calendar risk is higher than stock purchasing, so this should not block the first release.

**Rights and accuracy.** Obtain permission from the actual rights holder for derivative renders, CAD/motion inputs,
app/R2 distribution, offline retention and the intended commercial terms; confirm any studio subcontractor rights.
Negotiate continued use of delivered packs if the partnership ends. Manufacturer approval is valuable for the
machine mechanism but does not replace independent movement review. Branding should not imply an endorsement that
was never granted.

**Offline and custom exercises.** Contracted renders are bundled/cached like any other pack. Customs on that
mechanism can explicitly select its approved demo; another brand/model needs its own mapping or asset. Never infer
identical mechanics from a shared exercise name.

**First experiment.** Seek one written pilot grant for a specialty mechanism and require one render in our template.
Reject if permission is revocable on ordinary subscription cancellation, requires online-only viewing, bars our
rendering style, or cannot be traced to the asset owner. This proposal entails outreach later; none was sent here.

## 6. AI video as a tightly limited production experiment

**How and look.** Start with our own figure, equipment images and reviewer-approved pose brief. Generate short clips,
select a candidate, inspect the entire movement frame by frame, repair or discard it, then trim to a seamless loop
and store the final asset. Lock figure proportions, camera and lighting across inputs. Generate offline during
production; never synthesize a new instructional clip live from a tester's exercise name. Paid commercial-use terms
do not make an output mechanically correct.

**Coverage and cost.** In principle a model can attempt all 90; accepted coverage is unknown until every clip passes.
Machines are the critical weakness: changing lever length, hands sliding through handles, impossible hinges and
fading muscle highlights can look plausible at normal speed. Plan manual replacements for failures. As a cost
example, Runway's current API lists Gen-4 Turbo at 5 credits/second and credits at $0.01: a ten-second attempt is
$0.50. Ninety exercises with 5–20 attempts each would be **$225–$900**, before input images, other tools and review.
That is arithmetic, not a prediction of acceptance rate. [Runway API pricing](https://docs.dev.runwayml.com/guides/pricing/).
Allow another 60–160 hours for curation/cleanup, with no promise that the budget produces ninety usable loops.

**Rights.** Use only our original inputs or inputs explicitly permitting this AI use. Runway says it imposes no
noncommercial restriction on creations; this does not clear third-party material or confer exclusive rights to
every generated frame. [Runway commercial-use guidance](https://help.runwayml.com/hc/en-us/articles/21668707517587-Can-I-use-the-content-I-made-in-Runway-for-commercial-purposes).
US copyrightability still depends on sufficient human authorship, rather than prompts alone.
[US Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html).
Do not feed Gym visual assets into this pipeline. Assess the specific model's terms, not just an aggregator's plan.

**Offline and custom exercises.** Approved exports play offline identically to other media. New customs enter a
human-reviewed production queue; the app does not promise instant approved AI form. The highest-risk output should
remain unpublished, even if that leaves a gap.

**First experiment.** Try five original-input clips: bench, deadlift, leg extension, pec deck and pendulum squat.
Cap generation at twenty attempts per movement and $50 for video credits. Any unresolved dangerous/contact error
fails that clip; repeated machine failures kill this as a full-library strategy. A successful easy curl is not
proof. I rank this last as the authoritative source, though AI-assisted tooling/code can help options 2–3.

## Shared implementation in this repository

These are proposed components, not code already added.

**Separate media identity from workout history.** SeedExercise currently has stable UUID, name, load type, equipment
tags and muscle group. Start with a read-only Resources/ExerciseMediaManifest.json keyed by those UUIDs. SeedCatalog.json
then references media through its existing IDs; no rename matching and no new SwiftData field are needed for seeded
media. A future optional mediaKey in SeedExercise could point into that manifest, but it need not be copied onto
persisted Exercise rows. Keep catalog reconciliation and frozen workout snapshots unchanged.

A proposed record could look like this; it is a schema example, not an existing approved asset:

    {
      "exerciseID": "5EED0001-0000-4000-8000-000000000045",
      "variant": "seated-lever-leg-extension",
      "assetID": "stacked.leg-extension.v1",
      "poster": "stacked.leg-extension.v1.webp",
      "video": "stacked.leg-extension.v1.mp4",
      "durationSeconds": 6,
      "rightsRecordID": "owned-pack-1",
      "reviewRevision": 1
    }

Add hash, bytes, dimensions, attribution and approved equipment variants in the real manifest. A generic demo must
say which variation it depicts; use a machine-model override only after someone verifies that match. Resolve a
workout pop-up from the entry's exercise/snapshot context without rewriting that context. Media assignment must not
merge or alter D36 history groups.

**Keep source provenance and production tooling separate.** Original pose files might live in
Resources/ExerciseMotion/*.json and original Blender render scripts in scripts/exercise-media/. Those locations are
safe for public Git only for assets we are entitled and choose to publish. Purchased originals, restricted rigs,
contracts and access credentials belong in private storage, not the repo. The public repo can contain resolver code,
metadata permitted by its license and an original sample animation for contributors; build-time asset injection
can supply a licensed full pack. No third-party rendering engine has to ship in the app.

**One native player, two entry points.** A shared Features/ExerciseMedia view serves ExerciseDetailView and a
quick-dismiss sheet from the active workout. Use a poster in exercise lists and one silent loop on the visible
page/sheet; do not decode ninety animations in scrolling rows. AVFoundation provides
[AVPlayerLooper with AVQueuePlayer](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfoundation/avplayerlooper)
for local-file loops. Retain player/looper ownership, pause on dismissal/background, expose Play/Pause, and respect
Reduce Motion with a still/manual-play default. Silent playback must not interfere with the rest alarm or other
workout audio. For option 3, the same interface hosts the native diagram renderer.

**Choose an honest offline promise.** If the full approved pack fits, injecting it into the app bundle gives all
seeded demos on first launch without a network. Otherwise bundle posters plus a small core pack and provide explicit
“Download all”/pack download; play a verified local copy thereafter and keep deliberately downloaded packs across
relaunch. First use of an undownloaded clip remains online—say so. A six-second clip at 1 Mbps is about 0.75 MB,
so 90 would be about 67.5 MB before posters/container overhead; this is a sizing target, not a measured asset pack.
Benchmark quality and size before choosing GIFs or video.

For authorized R2 delivery, use our Worker to issue app-delivery URLs, with no storage credentials in the binary.
Keep a version/hash and last-good local file; handle interrupted downloads atomically. Signed URLs reduce casual
hotlinking but cannot make distributed video unextractable or override a license restriction. Media requests reveal
IP and requested asset to the delivery service, so update D61/privacy and App Privacy before remote delivery ships.
Do not log account-linked exercise viewing unnecessarily. No YouTube or AI API is needed at playback time.

**Custom associations are real user data.** Distinguish a read-only seeded manifest from a user's durable media
selection or imported clip. Plan persistence, deletion/export behavior and migration explicitly. The spec's Gate S
and ticket 11 obligations for user clips remain; moving files outside SwiftData is not a reason to skip those
obligations. Recording remains an optional future capability for other users, not a dependency on this user filming.

## My recommended next decision and proof

1. **Run the stock licensing/coverage pilot first**, before building a large animation system. Ask for a written
   ten-item offer and the exact distribution rights above. The verified specialty-machine listings make this more
   plausible than free motion packs, but they do not certify the catalog. Compare GIF and video source quality at
   real display size. Keep the map drawings ours and the commercial assets private.
2. **If that fails, choose the visual direction explicitly:** original 3D if the mannequin look is essential;
   original 2.5D if spending zero on asset licenses matters more. Prototype three mechanically different exercises,
   then estimate the rest from observed production time. Do not build both renderers into the first release.
3. **Define custom coverage honestly:** approved equivalent demo now; new movement queued for authoring. For a hard
   “every custom exercise always has animation” promise, require a reviewed movement choice during creation instead
   of guessing from free text.

For every pilot, use the same acceptance sheet: correct movement identity and machine path, stable contact points,
no impossible body/equipment motion, reviewer-approved useful range, loop continuity, readable target highlights,
phone-size clarity, offline relaunch and documented rights. Reject a clip for a material form error; do not average
that failure away with good aesthetics. These checks are about instructional quality, not a guarantee that one
universal technique fits every person's anatomy or machine setup.

I would not ship mixed wger photos, Everkinetic pose flips and unrelated 3D clips as the promised consistent animation
library. They can remain licensed supplementary references. The earlier free-source research is useful evidence
about coverage; it does not turn two still poses into verified intervening motion or a borrowed motion into an
owned one. The main choice is modest licensing expenditure versus substantial original authoring—not a hidden free
source that already solves all ninety machines and future custom movements.
